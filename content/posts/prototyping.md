What design questions did you intend to answer with your prototypes?

Our prototypes aimed to answer two questions: (1) Is the semester grid layout intuitive as a way to enter and visualize a four-year plan? (2) Does showing requirement completion status before vs. after a user action help or distract students from planning?

How did you design your prototypes and subsequent user studies to address these questions?

We built a paper prototype of the 4×2 semester grid (four columns for two semesters per academic year, two rows for upper and lower half of the page) and tested it with three students. For the live-vs-on-demand question we built two versions of the requirements panel — one that updated after every course added and one that only updated when the user clicked "Check Requirements" — and alternated which version each test user saw first. We asked users to think aloud while filling in a sample plan and observed where they paused, re-read, or expressed confusion.

What answers to your design questions did your efforts unveil?

The 4-column semester grid was immediately legible to all users — no one needed an explanation of what the columns and rows represented. The live-update version of the requirements panel, however, caused two out of three users to shift their attention away from course selection and toward the requirements checklist prematurely, leading them to make suboptimal sequence choices. The on-demand "Check Requirements" button design preserved their planning focus. This confirmed the final design direction.
